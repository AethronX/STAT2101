Probability and Counting Rules الاحتمال باستخدام قواعد العدّ

This lesson is where Chapter 4 comes together: the counting rules of §4-4 produce n(E) and n(S), and the classical probability formula of §4-1 divides them. Course-outline criterion CLO4-A — calculate probabilities using counting rules and basic probability principles — describes exactly this kind of question.

Tools carried over from §4-1 and §4-4

Classical probability:  P(E) = n(E) / n(S)

Combination:  nCr = n! / [(n − r)! · r!]

Fundamental counting rule:  k1 × k2 × ⋯ × kn

At least one:  P(at least one) = 1 − P(none)

Choosing from several categories ("2 defective and 2 good") → one combination per category, multiplied together.

1 · The three-step method

This is the destination. The counting rules supply n(E) and n(S); classical probability divides them. The structure of every §4-5 question is identical:

  1. STEP 1Count the sample space. How many total ways can the selection be made? Usually a single nCr or a product.
  2. STEP 2Count the event. How many of those ways satisfy the condition? Usually a product of smaller C's — one per category.
  3. STEP 3Divide. P(E) = n(E) / n(S), then round sensibly and interpret in words.
الشرح بالعربي — الاحتمال باستخدام قواعد العدّ (أهم نوع أسئلة)

كل سؤال من هذا النوع له نفس الخطوات الثلاث:

  1. احسب n(S): عدد كل طرق الاختيار الممكنة — غالبًا nCr واحدة.
  2. احسب n(E): عدد الطرق التي تحقق الشرط — غالبًا حاصل ضرب توافيق، واحدة لكل فئة.
  3. اقسم: P(E) = n(E) ÷ n(S)، ثم اكتب جملة تفسيرية.

مثال: 24 قطعة منها 4 معيبة، نختار 4. احتمال قطعتين معيبتين بالضبط = (4C2 × 20C2) ÷ 24C4 = 1140 ÷ 10626 ≈ 0.1073. وإذا طُلب «واحدة معيبة على الأقل» ← 1 − P(none) = 1 − 0.4560 = 0.5440.

قاعدة الاتساق: استخدم نفس نوع العدّ في البسط والمقام — لا تضع تباديل فوق توافيق.

Example 4-51 · Defective Integrated Circuits

A box contains 24 integrated circuits, 4 of which are defective. If 4 are sold at random, find: (a) exactly 2 are defective; (b) none is defective; (c) all are defective; (d) at least 1 is defective.

Set-up. 4 defective, 20 good, 24 total. Choosing 4 with no order → combinations.

n(S) = 24C4 = 24 · 23 · 22 · 214 · 3 · 2 · 1 = 255,02424 = 10,626

  1. (a) Exactly 2 defective — so 2 from the 4 defective and 2 from the 20 good:
    n(E) = 4C2 × 20C2 = 6 × 190 = 1,140
    P = 1,14010,626 ≈ 0.1073
  2. (b) None defective — all 4 from the 20 good:
    n(E) = 20C4 = 20 · 19 · 18 · 1724 = 4,845
    P = 4,84510,626 ≈ 0.4560
  3. (c) All defective — all 4 from the 4 defective:
    n(E) = 4C4 = 1
    P = 110,626 ≈ 0.000094
  4. (d) At least 1 defective — the complement of "none defective", which is part (b):
    P = 1 − 0.4560 = 0.5440
Part (d) is the exam's reward for recognising the complement

Done the long way, (d) = P(1 defective) + P(2) + P(3) + P(4) — four calculations. Done as 1 − P(none), it is one subtraction you have already computed. This is precisely why parts (b) and (d) appear in the same question: the examiner is testing whether you noticed.

Example 4-52 · Term Paper Selection

A student needs to select two topics to write two term papers for a course. There are 8 topics in economics and 11 topics in science. Find the probability that she selects one topic in economics and one topic in science.

  1. n(S): any 2 of the 19 available topics → 19C2 = 19 · 182 = 171
  2. n(E): one economics and one science → 8C1 × 11C1 = 8 × 11 = 88
  3. P = 88171 ≈ 0.5146
Example 4-53 · State Lottery Number

In the Pennsylvania State Lottery, a person selects a three-digit number and repetitions are permitted. If a winning number is selected, find the probability that it will have all three digits the same.

  1. n(S): three digits, repetition permitted → 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000
  2. n(E): all three digits the same → 000, 111, 222, …, 999 → 10
  3. P = 101,000 = 0.01  (1%)
Example 4-54 · Tennis Tournament

There are 8 married couples in a tennis club. If 1 man and 1 woman are selected at random to plan the summer tournament, find the probability that they are married to each other.

  1. n(S): 8 men to choose from and 8 women → 8 × 8 = 64 pairs
  2. n(E): only 8 of those pairs are actually married to each other (one per couple) → 8
  3. P = 864 = 18 = 0.125

Alternative reasoning that proves the answer: pick any man first; his wife is 1 of the 8 women, so P = 1/8 directly. ✔

HW 11 · Socks in a Drawer

A drawer contains 11 identical red socks and 8 identical black socks. Suppose you choose 2 socks at random in the dark. (a) What is the probability that you get a pair of red socks? (b) A pair of black socks? (c) 2 unmatched socks?

n(S): any 2 of 19 socks → 19C2 = 19 · 182 = 171

  1. (a) Pair of red: 11C2 = 11 · 102 = 55  →  P = 55171 ≈ 0.3216
  2. (b) Pair of black: 8C2 = 8 · 72 = 28  →  P = 28171 ≈ 0.1637
  3. (c) 2 unmatched: one red and one black: 11C1 × 8C1 = 88  →  P = 88171 ≈ 0.5146
    Or by complement: 1 − 0.3216 − 0.1637 = 0.5146 ✔

Check: 55 + 28 + 88 = 171 = n(S). The three cases are exhaustive and mutually exclusive, so their counts must total the sample space. ✔
