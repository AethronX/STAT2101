1 · Bar graph Both · CLO2-C

A bar graph represents data with vertical or horizontal bars whose heights or lengths are the frequencies (or relative frequencies, percentages, or the quantity measured). It is used for categorical (qualitative) data. Bars are equally spaced and do not touch, because the categories are separate rather than a continuous scale.

2 · Compound bar graphs Both

Bar graphs that compare data for two or more groups. A grouped (side-by-side) bar graph puts each group's bar next to the others; a stacked bar graph splits one column into sections, one per group.

Never-married US adults (millions) Ahmed's slides

Males 15.3, 24.2, 32.3, 40.2 and females 12.3, 20.2, 27.8, 34.0 in 1960, 1980, 2000, 2010.

Both groups rise steadily every period, and males outnumber females every year. The stacked totals are 27.6, 44.4, 60.1 and 74.2 million, so the total nearly tripled from 1960 to 2010.

3 · Pie graph Both

A circle divided into sections according to the percentage of frequencies in each category. Data are shown as percentages or proportions; the variable is usually nominal or categorical; the purpose is to show the relationship of the parts to the whole.

Steps

degrees = ⟪f‖n⟫ × 360°    % = ⟪f‖n⟫ × 100

Draw each sector with a protractor and compass, and label it with the name and percentage.

4 · Time series graph Both

Represents data at regular intervals over time, as a line graph; useful for monitoring change. When analysing one, look for trends (upward or downward) and note the slope to see how fast the change is.

Steps: (1) time on the x-axis, the variable on the y-axis, with a suitable scale; (2) plot a point at each time; (3) join the points with straight lines to show the trend; (4) add a title and label both axes. A compound time series graph draws two or more lines on one set of axes (e.g. the percentage of elderly men and women in the US labour force, 1960–2010). Ahmed's slides

5 · Stem-and-leaf plot Both

Shows numerical data arranged in order; for numeric data only. Each value is split into a stem (the leading digit or digits) and a leaf (usually the last digit), separated by "|". The plot needs a key to be readable (e.g. 2 | 5 means 25; 6 is recorded as 0 | 6). Its leaves act like horizontal histogram bars, so it shows the shape, and unlike a histogram it keeps every original value.

Steps: (1) arrange the data in ascending order; (2) choose the stems; (3) list the leaves beside each stem from smallest to largest, keeping a stem with no leaves; (4) give a key.

Decimals Amani: 12.3, 12.5, 13.0 becomes 12 | 3 5 and 13 | 0 with key 12 | 3 = 12.3; 1.23, 1.25, 1.30 becomes 1.2 | 3 5 and 1.3 | 0 with key 1.2 | 3 = 1.23. A stem with no data still appears (her example has an empty "5 |" row).
