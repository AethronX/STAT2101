1 · How data are collected Ahmed · CLO1-C

The most common method is the survey, done by telephone survey, mailed / emailed / online questionnaire, or personal interview. Data can also come from surveying records, direct observation, experiments, existing data (online or archived), census data, and simulation.

Dr. Ahmed's sample instrument, an "Academic Performance Scale", has 8 statements rated SA / A / N / D / SD and scored 5, 4, 3, 2, 1. Each item response is ordinal (a Likert scale), even though it is scored with numbers.

2 · Unbiased sampling: the four methods Both · CLO1-C

An unbiased sample gives each subject in the population an equally likely chance of being selected. The four methods are simple random, systematic, stratified and cluster.

2.1 Simple random sample (SRS)

Units are selected by chance, so every member of the population has a known, equal probability of selection. Three ways to select one: Amani

  1. Lottery method: number the population 1 to N, write each number on paper, mix in a bowl, draw n pieces without replacement. Disadvantages: uneven mixing causes bias; slow and impractical for large N.
  2. Random numbers by computer or calculator: generate n different random numbers between 1 and N, without replacement.
  3. Random-number table (Table D, Appendix A), used before computers.
Using Table D

(1) Number the population from 1 to N. (2) Fix the sample size n. (3) Start at any cell and move in one fixed direction (row by row or column by column). (4) Read numbers with the same number of digits as N (N = 950 → 3-digit groups 001–950). (5) Keep going until n different values are collected; discard any number greater than N, and any duplicate.

2.2 Systematic sample

Select every k-th member of a population arranged in some natural sequential order (a queue, a list of students, employee records), starting from a random point.

Steps

k = Nn  → round down to a whole number

(1) Arrange the population in order. (2) Compute k. (3) Choose the first subject at random from 1 to k. (4) Keep adding k: start, start + k, start + 2k, …

86 lemon trees along a fence, select 6 Both

N = 86, n = 6, k = 86/6 = 14.33, rounded down to k = 14. Random start between 1 and 14, say 6:

6, 20, 34, 48, 62, 76

Dr. Ahmed rounds up to k = 15 with start 8: 8, 23, 38, 53, 68, 83. That happens to work, but a start of 15 gives 15, 30, 45, 60, 75 and then 90 > 86: only five trees. Round down and you can never fall short.

Activity · sampling method Amani

(1) In a large Muscat school, all teachers from two buildings are interviewed about homework. (2) The Oman Traffic Authority groups drivers by experience (< 5, 5–10, > 10 years) and randomly selects drivers from each group. (3) A Muscat Grand Mall supermarket surveys every 10th customer at checkout. (4) The Ministry of Health randomly generates phone numbers from a national database and calls citizens.

(1) cluster: buildings are the clusters, and everyone in the chosen ones is included. (2) stratified: a random sample from every experience group. (3) systematic: every k-th with k = 10. (4) simple random: every number in the database has an equal chance.

3 · Other (non-random) sampling methods Ahmed

Convenience sample

Subjects who are convenient and readily available, easy to reach. Examples: the first 10 students entering class; interviewing people leaving a local hypermarket.

Volunteer (self-selected) sample

Respondents decide for themselves whether to be included. Examples: clinic patients who choose to fill a satisfaction survey; students who choose to complete a course evaluation.

Neither is an unbiased sample: both usually lead to a biased sample, because the people who are easy to reach, or who volunteer, often differ from the rest of the population.

4 · Sampling and nonsampling errors Ahmed

Sampling error

The difference between a sample result and the population result, caused only by chance in who was sampled. Example: 56% of a sample of full-time students are female, but the admissions office shows 54%. The 2% difference is sampling error.

Nonsampling error

The data are obtained incorrectly, or the sample is biased (nonrepresentative). Examples: instrument errors (a defective thermostat reading 1.3° off), data entry or recording errors, measuring errors.
