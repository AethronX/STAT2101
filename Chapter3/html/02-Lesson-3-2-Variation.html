Measures of Variation مقاييس التشتت — المدى والتباين والانحراف المعياري ومعامل الاختلاف ونظرية تشيبيشيف

Variation (spread, dispersion) describes how far the data values are from each other, or how dispersed they are. The main measures are the range, variance, standard deviation and interquartile range (covered in 3-3). A measure of variation combined with a measure of centre gives a good picture of a distribution. Both

0 · Why centre is not enough Amani

1 · The range Both

Range

R = highest value − lowest value

The simplest measure, but it depends only on the two extremes, so one extreme value changes it markedly. It is less useful than the variance and SD. Give it as a single number.

Simple example Amani · Ex 3-16 paint Hadhrami

(a) 1, 2, 4 · (b) 1, 2, 1000 · (c) months before fading: brand A 10 60 50 30 40 20, brand B 35 45 30 35 40 25.

(a) R = 3 · (b) R = 999: one extreme value multiplies the range by 333. (c) A: 60 − 10 = 50 months; B: 45 − 25 = 20 months, less than half of brand A's. Textbook: Ex 3-15, 3-17.

2 · Variance and standard deviation Both

Both are built on the deviation, the distance of each value from the mean: (X − μ) or (X − x̄). Deviations always sum to zero, Σ(X − x̄) = 0, so they are squared before averaging. The variance is the average of the squared deviations, and the standard deviation is its square root.

Population (parameter)Sample (statistic)
Varianceالتباينσ² = Σ(X − μ)²Ns² = Σ(X − x̄)²n − 1
Standard deviationالانحراف المعياريσ = √σ²s = √s²
Shortcut (sample)s² = n(ΣX²) − (ΣX)²n(n − 1)   or   s² = ΣX² − n·x̄²n − 1
Properties to quote

Variance and SD can never be negative, so take the positive square root. If the values sit close to the mean, the variance is small. The serious disadvantage of the variance is that its units are squared (units²). The SD has the same units as the data. The larger the SD, the more spread out the data. When two data sets have equal means, the one with the larger variance or SD is more variable. ΣX² (square, then sum) is not (ΣX)² (sum, then square).

Research question in both decks: why n − 1?

The sample mean x̄ is computed from the same data, so the values sit closer to x̄ than to the true μ. Dividing by n would therefore underestimate σ². Dividing by n − 1 (the degrees of freedom: once x̄ is fixed, only n − 1 deviations are free) gives a slightly larger value, which makes s² an unbiased estimator of σ².

4 · Coefficient of variation (CVar) Both

Coefficient of variation: SD as a percentage of the mean

sample: CVar = sx̄ · 100    population: CVar = σμ · 100

The SD and the mean share the same units, so CVar has no units. That is why it can compare standard deviations measured in different units, or data sets with different means. Lower CV: values closer together, less variable and more consistent. Higher CV: more spread out, greater relative variability.

Ex 3-23 · Car sales vs commissions Both

Sales: mean 87, s = 5. Commissions: mean 5225 OMR, s = 773.

sales 5/87 · 100 ≈ 5.7%   commissions 773/5225 · 100 ≈ 14.8%

The commissions are more variable than the sales.

Dept 1: 40 employees, mean 450 OMR, SD 7. Dept 2: 65 employees, mean 350 OMR, SD 9. (1) Which has the higher total salary expense? (2) Which has greater variability?

(1) The total is n·x̄: 40 × 450 = 18,000 and 65 × 350 = 22,750, so Dept 2.

5 · Range rule of thumb Both

Quick estimate of s, only for unimodal and roughly symmetric data

s ≈ range4

Why 4? (research question) For bell-shaped data about 95% of values lie within x̄ ± 2s, an interval of width 4s, so the range is roughly 4s. The textbook uses the same idea to approximate the smallest and largest values: about x̄ − 2s and x̄ + 2s.

Examples Amani · Hadhrami

(a) 12, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 18, 20, 20, 25. (b) 5, 8, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13.

(a) s ≈ 13/4 = 3.25, while the formula gives s = 4.06. (b) s ≈ 8/4 = 2, while the formula gives 2.7.

The rule is quick but less accurate. In most cases it underestimates s, but not always.

6 · Chebyshev's theorem: any shape Both

Chebyshev (1821–1894): for any k > 1, not necessarily an integer

at least  1 − 1k²  of the data lie within  x̄ ± k·s (sample)  or  μ ± k·σ (population)

k = 1.5: at least 55.56% · k = 2: at least 75% · k = 2.5: at least 84% · k = 3: at least 88.89% (8/9). Finding k: k = (upper limit − mean)/SD. The answer is always "at least", never "exactly".

Activity · n = 50, x̄ = 28, s = 3 Amani

What can be said about the number of observations (1) inside (22, 34) and (2) outside it?

28 − 3k = 22, so k = (28 − 22)/3 = 2, and 1 − 1/4 = 75%.

(1) 0.75 × 50 = 37.5: at least 38 observations inside. Round up for "at least".

(2) at most 12 outside (50 − 38).

7 · The empirical (normal) rule: bell-shaped only Hadhrami

When the distribution is bell-shaped (normal), approximately 68% of values lie within 1 SD of the mean, 95% within 2 SD, and 99.7% within 3 SD. Chapter 6 develops this further.

ChebyshevEmpirical
Applies toany shapebell-shaped only
Within 2 SDat least 75%about 95%
Within 3 SDat least 88.89%about 99.7%
Wording"at least""approximately"

Example: national exam scores with mean 480 and SD 90, normally distributed. About 68% lie between 390 and 570 (480 ± 90), 95% between 300 and 660 (480 ± 180), and 99.7% between 210 and 750 (480 ± 270).

8 · Uses of variance and SD · Table 3-2 Both

(1) They describe the spread of data and compare which of two or more data sets is more variable. (2) They measure the consistency of a variable: in manufacturing nuts and bolts, the variation in diameters must be small or the parts will not fit. (3) They give the number of values that fall within an interval (Chebyshev). (4) They are used heavily in inferential statistics in later chapters.
